# The machine learning behind the system

From "what is learning?" to the exact network, loss, optimiser and training loop the project uses, followed by the extra components: quality gate, Grad-CAM, lesion segmentation, the rule engine, ensembles and robustness training.

## What is machine learning?

### Programming versus learning

In ordinary programming a person writes the rules: *if the image has more than 20 red dots in each quadrant, call it severe*. For fundus photographs nobody can write such rules reliably: what counts as a "red dot" depends on lighting, camera, vessels crossing and many subtle cues.

**Machine learning** reverses the process. Instead of writing rules, we collect many examples where the answer is known and let an algorithm adjust a flexible mathematical function until its outputs match the answers. In **supervised learning** each example is a pair (input, target): here, (photograph, grade). The adjustable numbers inside the function are its **parameters** or **weights**; adjusting them is **training**; using the trained function on new inputs is **inference** ({{ref:supervised}}).

{{fig:supervised.png|Supervised learning: training happens once, offline; inference happens for every new photograph (original diagram).|94|supervised}}

::: example Learning a rule from examples
Suppose we want to predict whether a fruit is ripe from its colour (a number from 0, green, to 1, red) and have ten fruits labelled ripe or unripe. A learning algorithm can try thresholds and keep the one that misclassifies the fewest examples, say 0.6. Nobody told it "0.6"; it learned the value from data. A neural network does the same thing with millions of parameters instead of one, and with features it discovers itself instead of a single colour value.
:::

### Generalising versus memorising

The goal is not to fit the training examples but to perform well on **new** ones. A flexible model can simply memorise the training set, including its noise, and then fail on new images. This is **over-fitting**. A model that is too simple or trained too briefly misses real patterns; this is **under-fitting**. We detect over-fitting by watching performance on the validation set, which the model never trains on ({{ref:fitting}}).

{{fig:fitting_illustration.png|Under- and over-fitting (illustrative curves). Training loss keeps falling; validation loss falls, then rises once the model starts memorising. The project keeps the weights from the best validation epoch.|62|fitting}}

## Neural networks and convolution

### The artificial neuron

A neural network is built from simple units. Each **neuron** multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up with a bias, and passes the sum through a non-linear **activation function** $f$:

$$ y = f\left(\sum_{i} w_i\, x_i + b\right) $$

where $x_i$ are the inputs, $w_i$ the weights, $b$ the bias and $y$ the output. Without the non-linearity, stacking layers would collapse into a single linear function, unable to represent complex patterns. EfficientNet uses the **SiLU** (also called Swish) activation [46], $\mathrm{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \sigma(z)$, where $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$; it behaves like the popular ReLU ($\max(0,z)$) but is smooth.

A network arranges neurons in **layers**: the outputs of one layer are the inputs of the next. With enough layers and weights, such a network can approximate very complicated functions, and training finds weights that make it approximate the function from photographs to grades [12].

### Why images need convolution

Connecting every pixel of a 384 × 384 × 3 image to every neuron of a layer would need hundreds of millions of weights for one layer, and the network would have to learn separately that a microaneurysm in the top-left corner is the same thing as one in the bottom-right. A **convolutional neural network** (CNN) [13] solves both problems: it slides a small **filter** (a 3 × 3 or 5 × 5 grid of weights) over the image and computes, at every position, the sum of products of the filter and the pixels under it:

$$ Y[i,j] = \sum_{u}\sum_{v} K[u,v]\; X[i+u,\ j+v] + b $$

Here $X$ is the input, $K$ the filter, $b$ a bias and $Y$ the output, called a **feature map**. The same few weights are reused everywhere (*weight sharing*), and a pattern is detected wherever it occurs.

{{fig:convolution.png|Convolution worked by hand (illustrative numbers). A vertical-edge filter produces large values exactly where dark pixels meet bright ones.|100|conv}}

::: how Stride, padding, channels and pooling
- **Stride** is the step size of the sliding filter. A stride of 2 halves the height and width of the output.
- **Padding** adds a border of zeros so that the filter can be centred on edge pixels.
- The output size is $o = \lfloor (n + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1$ for input size $n$, filter size $k$, padding $p$ and stride $s$. EfficientNet's first layer has $n = 384$, $k = 3$, $p = 1$, $s = 2$, giving $o = 192$, which matches the measured shape in {{ref:effnet}}.
- **Channels**: a layer uses many filters at once, each producing its own feature map; the stack of maps is the layer's output (for example 32 channels after the first layer).
- **Pooling** summarises a region by its average or maximum. EfficientNet ends with *global average pooling*, which averages each of its 1,280 final feature maps to a single number.
:::

Early layers learn simple filters (edges, colour contrasts); deeper layers combine them into textures, blobs and vessel-like structures; the deepest layers respond to complex, task-specific patterns. {{ref:fmaps}} shows feature maps of the project's trained network for a real image.

{{fig:feature_maps.png|Real feature maps of the project's trained EfficientNet-B0 for one APTOS image: six channels after the first layer (192 × 192) and four from stage 3 (48 × 48). Early maps look like edge and vessel detectors; deeper maps are coarser and more abstract.|100|fmaps}}

Two further building blocks appear in every modern CNN. **Batch normalisation** [45] rescales each channel's values inside a batch to a standard range, which makes training faster and more stable. **Dropout** [44] randomly sets a fraction of values to zero during training (the project uses 30% before the final layer), so the network cannot rely on any single feature.

## The model: EfficientNet-B0 and transfer learning

### The architecture

The project's main classifier is **EfficientNet-B0** [16], loaded from the timm library [38] with a new final layer of five outputs (one per grade). EfficientNet was designed by scaling a small base network in depth, width and input resolution together, and B0 is the smallest member of the family. It is a good fit for a dataset of a few thousand images and a free cloud GPU.

{{fig:efficientnet_b0.png|EfficientNet-B0 as used in the project. Every shape below a block was measured by passing a real preprocessed image through the trained checkpoint (format: channels × height × width).|100|effnet}}

Its main building block is the **MBConv** block from MobileNetV2 [17] ({{ref:mbconv}}): a cheap 1 × 1 convolution expands the number of channels, a *depthwise* convolution filters each channel separately with a 3 × 3 or 5 × 5 filter, a **squeeze-and-excitation** step [18] learns to re-weight channels by their global importance, and a 1 × 1 convolution projects back to fewer channels. When the input and output shapes match, the input is added to the output (a *skip connection*), which helps training deep networks.

{{fig:mbconv.png|The MBConv block (original diagram).|94|mbconv}}

The trained network has about **4.0 million parameters**: seven stages of MBConv blocks (1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 1 blocks) that reduce a 384 × 384 image to 1,280 feature maps of 12 × 12, global average pooling to 1,280 numbers, dropout, and a final linear layer to five scores.

### Transfer learning

::: define Pre-trained weights and fine-tuning
**Transfer learning** starts from a network already trained on a large, general dataset, here the ImageNet collection of more than a million everyday photographs in 1,000 categories [15], and continues training it on the new task. **Fine-tuning** means updating the pre-trained weights with a small learning rate. A *frozen* layer is one whose weights are not updated.
:::

Why should a network trained on cats and cars help with retinas? Its early layers have learned generic visual features, such as edges, blobs, textures and colour contrasts, that are useful for almost any image [47]. Starting from them, the network needs far fewer medical images to learn the new task than starting from random weights. The project fine-tunes **all** layers (none are frozen), replaces only the 1,000-way ImageNet layer with a fresh 5-way layer, and also trains a small CNN from scratch as a baseline. The pre-trained model reached a quadratic weighted kappa of {{val:baselines.EfficientNet-B0 transfer learning (384px).qwk|.3f}} on the test split against {{val:baselines.Small CNN from scratch (224px).qwk|.3f}} for the scratch network (Part VI).

::: project In our project
`drscreen/models.py`, `build_model("efficientnet_b0", 5, pretrained=True, drop_rate=0.3)` creates the network with timm; `load_checkpoint` rebuilds it from a saved `best.pt`. A second backbone, **EfficientNet-B3** at 448 pixels (about 10.7 million parameters), was trained the same way and combined with B0 in the ensemble described at the end of this part.
:::

## From scores to probabilities

### Logits, softmax and the predicted grade

The network's final layer outputs five unbounded numbers, the **logits** $z_0, \dots, z_4$, one per grade. The **softmax** function turns them into five positive numbers that sum to 1:

$$ p_k = \frac{e^{z_k / T}}{\sum_{j=0}^{4} e^{z_j / T}} $$

with temperature $T = 1$ during training. The **predicted grade** is the grade with the largest $p_k$ (the *argmax*), and the **referable-DR score** used for screening is the total probability of the referable grades:

$$ P(\mathrm{referable}) = p_2 + p_3 + p_4 $$

{{fig:softmax.png|From logits to probabilities (illustrative numbers). Temperature scaling softens the probabilities without changing the most likely grade.|100|softmax}}

::: example Working through softmax
Take illustrative logits $z = (2.1, 0.3, 3.0, 0.8, -0.5)$. Exponentials: $e^{2.1} = 8.17$, $e^{0.3} = 1.35$, $e^{3.0} = 20.09$, $e^{0.8} = 2.23$, $e^{-0.5} = 0.61$; their sum is 32.45. Dividing gives $p \approx (0.25, 0.04, 0.62, 0.07, 0.02)$. The predicted grade is 2, and $P(\mathrm{referable}) = 0.62 + 0.07 + 0.02 = 0.71$.
:::

### Calibration by temperature scaling

Modern networks tend to be **over-confident**: when they say 95%, they are right less often than 95% of the time [22]. **Temperature scaling** fixes this with a single number $T$, fitted *after* training on the validation set so that the scaled probabilities match the observed outcomes as well as possible (it minimises the negative log-likelihood on validation images). $T > 1$ softens the probabilities; it never changes which grade is most likely. The project's fitted value was $T$ = {{val:validation_threshold_selection.temperature|.2f}}. How well this worked is measured in Part V and Part VI.

::: warning Score, probability and confidence are different things
- The **predicted grade** is the most likely class.
- The **referable-DR score** is a number between 0 and 1 produced by the model and temperature-scaled on one validation set. It behaves like a probability *for images like the validation images*, but it is not a verified probability of disease for a new camera or population.
- The **confidence level** shown in the interface (low, moderate, high, borderline) is a *heuristic*: how far the score lies from the decision threshold, lowered for poor image quality or disagreement with the lesion evidence. It is not the probability that the answer is right.
:::

## How the network learns

### Measuring error: the loss function

Training needs a single number that says how wrong the network is: the **loss**. For classification the standard choice is **cross-entropy**: for one image whose true grade is $y$, the loss is minus the logarithm of the probability the model gave to that grade, multiplied in this project by a class weight $w_y$:

$$ L = -\,w_y \,\ln p_y $$

If the model gives the right grade probability 0.9, the loss is $-\ln 0.9 = 0.11$; at 0.5 it is 0.69; at 0.1 it is 2.30 ({{ref:ce}}). Confident mistakes are punished heavily.

{{fig:cross_entropy.png|Cross-entropy loss as a function of the probability assigned to the correct grade.|60|ce}}

**Class imbalance.** Almost half of the training images are grade 0. Without correction the network can score well by favouring the common grades. The project gives rarer grades a larger weight, $w_k \propto (1/n_k)^{0.5}$ for a grade with $n_k$ training images, normalised so the weights average 1 (`train.class_weights`, power 0.5). The square root is a compromise: full inverse-frequency weighting would make the five grade-3 mistakes in a batch dominate everything. Over a batch, PyTorch averages the weighted losses (divided by the sum of the weights).

### Gradients and back-propagation

To reduce the loss we need to know, for every weight, which direction of change lowers it. That is the **gradient** $\partial L / \partial w$: the slope of the loss with respect to the weight. **Gradient descent** takes a small step downhill:

$$ w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} $$

where $\eta$ is the **learning rate**, the step size. Too small and training crawls; too large and it overshoots and diverges ({{ref:gd}}).

{{fig:gradient_descent.png|Gradient descent on a one-weight loss surface (illustrative). With a suitable learning rate the steps settle into a minimum; with a learning rate that is too large they jump around.|94|gd}}

A network has millions of weights, and **back-propagation** [14] computes all their gradients efficiently with the chain rule of calculus: starting from the loss, it passes "how much did this value contribute to the error" backwards through the layers, reusing intermediate results. PyTorch [37] does this automatically when the code calls `loss.backward()`.

### The optimiser and the learning-rate schedule

The project uses **AdamW** [19, 20]. *Adam* keeps a running average of each weight's recent gradients (momentum) and of their squared size, and scales each step accordingly, so weights with small, noisy gradients still move sensibly. The *W* stands for *decoupled weight decay*: every step also shrinks the weights slightly (weight decay 0.01), a form of regularisation that discourages extreme weights.

The learning rate is not constant. It rises linearly from almost zero during the first epoch (**warm-up**, which avoids destroying the pre-trained weights with large early steps) and then follows a **cosine** curve down to 1% of its peak [21] ({{ref:lr}}). The peak learning rate was {{val:run.config.lr|.4f}}.

{{fig:lr_schedule.png|The actual learning-rate schedule of the main training run, computed from its configuration (peak 3 × 10⁻⁴, one warm-up epoch, cosine decay over 30 planned epochs). Training stopped early, at the dashed line.|64|lr}}

### Batches, epochs and the training loop

Training processes images in **batches** (24 at a time here) rather than one by one, which is faster on a GPU and gives smoother gradient estimates. One pass over all training images is an **epoch** (about 108 batches). The order of images is **shuffled** every epoch so that batches differ.

{{fig:training_loop.png|The project's training loop as implemented in `drscreen/train.py` (original diagram).|100|loop}}

```text
for epoch in 1..30:
    for each batch of 24 training images:          # shuffled, augmented on the fly
        logits = model(images)                     # forward pass (mixed precision on GPU)
        loss   = weighted_cross_entropy(logits, grades)
        loss.backward()                            # back-propagation: all gradients
        clip gradient norm to 5; AdamW step; update learning rate
    evaluate on validation images -> quadratic weighted kappa (QWK)
    save last.pt; if QWK improved, save best.pt
    stop if QWK has not improved for 8 epochs, or the time budget would be exceeded
```

::: define Regularisation and early stopping
**Regularisation** is any technique that discourages memorisation. The project combines four: augmentation, dropout (30%), weight decay (0.01) and **early stopping**, which ends training when the validation metric has not improved for a set number of epochs (8) and keeps the best epoch's weights. Two numerical safeguards complete the loop: **gradient clipping** limits the size of the gradient vector to 5 to prevent rare explosive steps, and **mixed precision** [43] runs most arithmetic in 16-bit floats on the GPU for speed. The code also stops with an error if the loss becomes infinite or not-a-number.
:::

## Components beyond the classifier

The classifier answers "how severe?". Five further components make the system usable and explainable. Each is explained only as deeply as the project needs.

### The image-quality gate

Not every photograph can be graded. The gate (`drscreen/quality.py`) computes simple measurements on a size-normalised copy of the image:

- **Sharpness**: the variance of the Laplacian (a second-derivative filter that responds to edges) on the green channel inside the retina. Blur removes edges, so the variance drops.
- **Brightness and contrast**: the mean and standard deviation of grey levels inside the retina.
- **Saturation**: the fraction of nearly white pixels (over-exposure).
- **Field of view**: whether a retina is found at all and how complete its circle is.

The thresholds are set from the distribution of these measurements on **800 training images**: an image is **rejected** (ask for a retake) if, for example, it is sharper than only 1% of training images, and **enhanced** with CLAHE if it is darker or lower in contrast than 95% of them. Otherwise it is **good**.

::: warning A heuristic, not a trained quality model
No dataset with genuine quality labels was used. The gate was evaluated only on synthetic degradations (Part VI): it catches blur and darkness well but is blind to JPEG compression and over-exposure. The robustness-augmented classifier was built partly to cover those gaps.
:::

### Grad-CAM: where did the classifier look?

A CNN's decision is a long chain of multiplications that no one can read directly. **Grad-CAM** [23] produces a coarse heat map of the image regions that most increased the score of the predicted grade ({{ref:gcamhow}}). It takes the last convolutional feature maps $A^k$ (1,280 maps of 12 × 12), computes how much the class score $y^c$ changes when each map changes (the gradient), averages those gradients over the map to get one importance weight per map, and adds the maps up with those weights:

$$ \alpha_k = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}}, \qquad L^c = \mathrm{ReLU}\left( \sum_k \alpha_k A^k \right) $$

where $Z = 144$ is the number of positions. The ReLU keeps only positive evidence. The 12 × 12 map is enlarged to the image size and overlaid in colour.

{{fig:gradcam_how.png|How Grad-CAM is computed (original diagram).|100|gcamhow}}

{{fig:gradcam_examples.png|Grad-CAM overlays from the training run for correct and incorrect test predictions of each grade (titles give the dataset grade and the prediction).|100|gcam}}

::: warning What Grad-CAM can and cannot show
Grad-CAM shows where the classifier's evidence came from, at a resolution of 12 × 12 cells. It is **not** a lesion detector, it cannot outline a microaneurysm, and a heat map can look plausible even when the reasoning is wrong. Following the sanity checks of Adebayo et al. [24], the project compared heat maps of the trained network with those of a network whose weights were randomised: their mean Spearman correlation was {{val:gradcam.randomization_spearman_mean|.2f}} on three images. The trained maps depend on what the network learned, which is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a meaningful explanation.
:::

### Lesion segmentation and the optic-disc / fovea locator

To give *reasons* for a grade, the project adds a lesion pathway ({{ref:lesionpipe}}). It was trained on IDRiD, the only dataset with pixel-level masks for all four lesion types.

{{fig:lesion_pipeline.png|The lesion pathway (original diagram). It runs beside the classifier and produces findings, quadrant counts and an ICDR rule grade with reasons.|100|lesionpipe}}

- **Segmentation** means predicting a label for every pixel instead of one label per image. The network is a **U-Net** [25]: an encoder (an EfficientNet-B0, pre-trained on ImageNet) that reduces the image to coarse, rich features, and a decoder that enlarges them back to full resolution, with *skip connections* that copy fine detail from the encoder to the decoder. It outputs five probability maps (microaneurysms, haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots, optic disc) on a 1,280-pixel canvas, trained on random 512-pixel patches.
- The maps are **thresholded** (thresholds chosen on validation images) and split into **connected regions**; regions smaller than a minimum area are dropped.
- A second small network regresses the **optic-disc and fovea centres**. Because it proved unreliable on APTOS images, the deployed locator takes the disc from the segmentation map and accepts the regressed fovea only if it is consistent; otherwise it places the fovea by an anatomical rule (about 2.5 disc diameters from the disc).
- Two axes through the fovea divide the retina into four **quadrants** (superior/inferior, nasal/temporal); regions are counted per quadrant and near the macula.

Segmentation quality is measured with overlap scores (Dice, IoU) and the area under the precision-recall curve; Part V explains them and Part VI reports them.

### The ICDR rule engine

The rule engine (`drscreen/rules.py`) turns the lesion counts into an ICDR grade using the criteria of {{ref:icdr}} and records, for each criterion, whether it is *met*, *not met* or *not assessed*, with the numbers behind it. Two sets of thresholds exist:

- **Literal**: the clinical wording (for example, more than 20 haemorrhages in each of four quadrants). On APTOS this calls almost everything referable, because the detector finds many more *regions* than a clinician would count *lesions* (fragments, false positives).
- **Data-calibrated** (used by the application): count thresholds fitted on APTOS training images by coordinate descent to best match the expert grades. For the deployed model they were {{val:lesion_v2_comparison.aptos_v1.rule_params.min_ma|d}} microaneurysm regions, {{val:lesion_v2_comparison.aptos_v1.rule_params.min_he|d}} haemorrhage regions, {{val:lesion_v2_comparison.aptos_v1.rule_params.min_ex|d}} exudate regions and {{val:lesion_v2_comparison.aptos_v1.rule_params.he_per_quadrant|d}} haemorrhage regions per quadrant, values that only make sense for this detector and are labelled as such in the interface.

Because there are no detectors for venous beading, IRMA or new vessels, the rule engine can never assign grade 4 and marks those criteria *not assessed*.

### Combining the evidence: cross-check and fusion

The classifier and the rule engine are compared on every image (`fusion.cross_check`):

- **agree**: both say referable, or both say not referable;
- **lesions only**: the rules say referable but the classifier does not, so the case goes to **human review** (a possible missed case);
- **classifier only**: the classifier flags referable disease but the detected lesions do not meet the rules' referable criteria; the referral stands and the lack of corroboration is reported.

A *fusion* model that combines the classifier's probabilities with lesion features was also built and evaluated (Part VI). It did not improve screening accuracy, so the lesion pathway is used to **explain** and to **cross-check**, not to change the score.

### Ensembles, test-time augmentation and robustness training

- An **ensemble** averages the predictions of several models; their errors partly cancel. The project's ensemble averages the temperature-scaled grade probabilities of the robust B0 and the robust B3.
- **Test-time augmentation (TTA)** averages a model's outputs on the image and on its horizontally and vertically mirrored copies.
- **Robustness training** is the stronger augmentation of {{ref:aug}} (blur, JPEG, illumination defects), used to train separate models.

Which of these became the default was decided by rules written down **before** the results were seen, using the validation set for the selection (`scripts/evaluate_ensemble.py`, `scripts/compare_robust.py`). The outcome is reported in Part VI.

### The workflow simulation

Finally, `sim/screening_simulation.py` uses the SimPy discrete-event library [40] (with queue lengths checked against Little's law [41]) to model a screening service: patients arriving, queueing for cameras, imaging, upload, AI analysis, recapture and human review. It uses the measured sensitivity, specificity and gate rates of the model, but every operational quantity (arrival rate, imaging time, number of cameras and reviewers) is an **assumption**. It illustrates where queues would form under those assumptions; it is not evidence about a real service.
